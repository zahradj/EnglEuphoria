## Design rules we should adopt

1. **Plan for dose, not for a single hour.** Tutoring effects rise with sessions per week (>= 3 days/week in the pooled data; higher weekly hours in the Italian RCT). Make Daily 10 the 3rd-to-5th "touch" each week, and tell families 2 live lessons + Daily 10 is the recommended rhythm. Confidence: medium (dose evidence is from schools and maths, not L2).
2. **Keep the structured feedback loop (Take 1 -> specific feedback -> Take 2) and the teacher's error log.** Structured feedback and mastery were most of Bloom's effect; feedback d about 0.5 but depends on content. Confidence: medium-high.
3. **Design every segment around a task change plus feedback, not around an "attention span".** The 10-minute claim is weak. Our run of show is fine for other reasons (variety, retrieval). Confidence: medium.
4. **Space retrieval on calendar days, with the gap proportional to the retention goal** (Cepeda logic; Kim & Webb overall benefit). Keep +1/+3/+7/+21 as a hypothesis and log which interval works. Confidence: medium (L2 lag evidence mixed).
5. **Production-first retrieval with feedback in Remember? and Daily 10, delayed check by item not by session score.** Consistent with testing effect (prior doc), mastery gates (about 0.5), and delayed-gain pattern in feedback research. Confidence: medium-high.
6. **Treat games as story/fiction and challenge, not as ranking.** Fiction and choice are plausible moderators (Sailer & Homner); leaderboards help top ranks, risk lower ones. No public ranking; streak freezes; streak never counts as progress. Confidence: medium.
7. **Use SDT-style design: choice within structure, clear rubric, competence feedback.** Autonomy-support training d about 0.63 and structure+autonomy reinforce each other. Confidence: medium.
8. **Low-stakes, retryable speaking with private rehearsal.** Anxiety r about -0.35 with performance; intervention effect unknown, so treat as rational design and measure. Confidence: medium (design) / low (effect).
9. **Use can-do self-ratings + a visible goal each Season, and report them as a habit-builder, not as a score lift.** Self-assessment: SRL/self-efficacy effects are documented, L2 score effect is not. Confidence: low-medium.
10. **Judge ourselves with delayed, production-based pilot data against L2 effect-size norms** (Plonsky & Oswald), expecting classroom effects to be smaller than lab. Confidence: high (method).
11. **Protect tutor quality and fidelity.** Effects fall as programmes scale and with non-professional tutors. Train and observe tutors; use the blueprint tightly. Confidence: medium-high.
12. **Keep explicit instruction (rule + practice) as the core for 14+; keep input and noticing for 11-13.** Adolescents' classroom rate is fast because of explicit learning ability (Munoz). Confidence: medium.

## Honest progress claims we may and may not make

**May say**
- "One-to-one tutoring is among the best-supported ways to raise learning in school research (pooled average about 0.3 SD in reading/maths trials), especially when frequent and structured."
- "Each lesson is built on retrieval, spacing and specific feedback, which have consistent positive effects in research."
- "Cambridge's guideline is about 200 guided learning hours per CEFR level; calendar time depends on lessons per week, practice between lessons and the learner." Show a transparent estimate: e.g. one 60-min lesson/week + Daily 10 (about 70 min/week of guided time) would take about 3 years per level at the Cambridge guide figure; two lessons/week about 1.5 years [arithmetic, ours; not a promise].
- "We measure progress with delayed speaking/writing samples and show them to you."
- "Teens are fast classroom learners compared with young children in the short term" (Munoz; with the reminder that exposure hours matter most).

**May not say**
- Any "2x faster", "2 sigma", "learn X times faster", or "reach B1 in N weeks/Seasons" claim.
- That our product, or any online 1:1 English product, has been shown to work in a controlled study for teens (none found).
- That gamification, leaderboards, streaks or anxiety-reduction features raise scores by a stated amount.
- That a certain number of minutes per day, hours per week, or talk-time % is proven best.
- That a 10-minute attention limit drives our segment lengths.
- Any figure marked [S2], [M] or [U] without opening the original.

## What the evidence does NOT support / where it is thin

- No controlled studies of online 1:1 English tutoring for teens with proficiency outcomes; the transfer from school-subject tutoring to L2 is an assumption.
- No data on teacher/learner talk ratio vs gain; no data on ideal lesson length for teens in L2; wait-time evidence is old/observational.
- Homework dose for L2 adolescents: nothing specific. Homework meta-analyses are correlational and mostly maths/science.
- App retention/engagement for teens: company data only; no independent RCT on reminders or streaks; streaks may build dependency.
- Gamification in L2: small, heterogeneous meta-analyses with publication-bias risk; no leaderboard or speaking split found.
- Anxiety: strong correlation, no pooled intervention evidence found.
- Self-assessment and goal setting: effects on SRL are documented; on L2 test scores they are not. Hattie syntheses are mixed-quality rankings.
- Spacing: most L2 data are vocabulary and lab; optimal gaps for grammar/speaking are unknown; the lag effect in L2 is mixed.
- Hours-to-level: Cambridge numbers are guidelines for adults and teens, not predictions; no adolescent-specific speed benchmark found.
