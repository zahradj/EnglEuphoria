## 2. Owner decisions that shape this build

- 2026-10-09: the lesson must fill an hour and reach real objectives. "Introduce themselves" was chosen as the aim. The blueprint ceiling "repeat, substitute, answer, ask" allows it **with sentence frames and no new items**: the student repeats and substitutes into "Hello, my name is ___. Nice to meet you." and asks "What is your name?" using only the 14 words and closed-class words. No item-bank change is needed (this replaces my first proposal to swap words).
- Cast is Vee, Ava, Theo only.
- Colour/logo work is separate (logo dropped).

## 3. The lesson goals (written, testable)

By the end the student can:
1. **Understand** the main idea of the profile mystery: who Sam is, what is odd about the profile, and say it in their own words (blueprint objective, unlocks clue 1).
2. **Recognise** the 14 words in text, and tell which words they already knew (teacher baseline).
3. **Say and write** "Hello, my name is ___. Nice to meet you." and ask "What is your name?" (frame use, repeat + substitute + ask).
4. **Give an opinion** about Sam in one sentence ("I think Sam is ___").
5. **Fill a small profile card** of their own (name or alias, student / teacher / friend, theme), shown back to them.

## 4. Minute budget: what fills the hour

A live lesson is teacher plus student on one stage. Teacher talk ≤ 40% means about 36 of the 60 minutes are student activity on screen; the rest is the teacher talking with the student around those screens (check-in chat, feedback ladder, Take 2 conversation). Every segment below states its **screen minutes (lower bound)** and the screens that fill it. A test adds estimated seconds per screen and fails below the lower bound or above 115% of the segment's minutes.

| Segment | Min | Screen min ≥ | Content |
|---|---|---|---|
| Check-in | 5 | 2 | Cast greet; student gives a name or alias and picks a theme and an energy emoji; Theo and Ava answer using the name ("Nice to meet you, {name}"). |
| Remember? | 7 | 4 | First lesson, nothing to recall: "What English do you already know?" sort of 12 word cards (I know it / not sure yet), then 3 quick retrieval questions. Baseline for the teacher. |
| The Drop | 10 | 7 | The mystery. Setup lines; a class group chat (about 10 messages, tap-to-gloss); **profile card with 3 hotspots to spot what is odd**; comic strip (5 panels); 4 gist checks; prediction "Who is Sam?" in the student's own words (clue 1). |
| Notice & Build | 10 | 8 | 12 words in 3 sets of 4 (meet, check); notice the frames "My name is ___" / "I am a ___"; match question to answer (6 pairs); sentence builder (2). |
| Energiser | 4 | 3 | Would-you-rather (theme); odd-one-out (2); quick "Sam or not Sam" true/false (3). No stakes. |
| Mission | 12 | 6 | Pick Chill / Normal / Push. Meet Theo: Take 1 (say or type your introduction), feedback ladder, Take 2 (add the question "What is your name?"). Then complete Sam's profile from the chat evidence (3 checks) and give an opinion about Sam. |
| Release | 7 | 3 | The student's own profile card (form), shown back as a card; two-sentence introduction (say or type, private first); one-sentence reaction "I think Sam is ___". |
| Wrap | 5 | 2 | Best Line, 3 can-do ticks, pick next theme, homework, clue 1 card, next clue teaser. |

## 5. Research behind the design (searched 2026-10-09; details in `docs/research/academy-a1s01e1-research.md`)

Three benchmarks, mechanic taken (never content), and how this lesson beats it:

1. **Fluentize "Personal introduction" (A1)** and **Twinkl/PPLI social-profile tasks**: a profile card is the context; students predict, check, then make their own profile. Taken: predict → check → create-your-own-card. Better: the profile is the mystery (spot what is odd), and the student's own card is shown back to them.
2. **Twee "First Lesson for A1: Nice to Meet You" and a standard 1:1 first-lesson plan**: rapport warm-up, read about people with comprehension questions, then dialogues. Taken: low-stakes rapport and a baseline. Better: the baseline is a 12-card self-sort that gives the teacher word-level data.
3. **Duolingo Stories**: comprehension questions inside the story, tap-to-translate on words, arrange-the-sentence and match tasks. Taken: question-inside-the-story gist checks, tap-to-gloss, tile builder, match. Better: the chat is a real group chat with named cast members and an unlocking clue, and nothing is scored publicly.

Evidence used: lexical coverage ≥ 95% is a minimum and about 98% is the target for unassisted comprehension (Hu & Nation 2000, Laufer 1989, Schmitt, Jiang & Grabe 2011) → the Drop glosses every new word and adds almost no other unknown words; glossed texts help word learning more than comprehension, so gist checks are kept separate from word meet (gloss meta-analysis); for beginners pre-teaching words helps listening more than schema activation (a Japanese high-school EFL study) → words are met in sets of 4 immediately after the Drop; task repetition mostly improves fluency (Bygate) → Take 2 repeats the same task with one addition; gist before detail.

Limits of this research (stated, not hidden): sources on first-lesson design for teens are practitioner material, not trials; coverage studies used intermediate learners; Duolingo's own design papers were not found, only teardowns and an unofficial format wiki.
