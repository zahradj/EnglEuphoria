# Writing and reviewing multiple-choice English test items: practical checklist

## 1. Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002) / Haladyna & Rodriguez (2013): concrete rules

### Takeaway
The validated taxonomy has 31 guidelines (content, format, stem, options), drawn from 27 textbooks and 27 studies. The best-supported rules are: one unambiguous key, a stem that carries the main idea, homogeneous options of similar length, plausible distractors based on real learner errors, no grammatical or length cues, 3 options are enough, and care with NOTA, "all of the above" (AOTA) and negatives.

### Cited Findings
- The taxonomy of 31 MC guidelines was validated against 27 textbooks and 27 research studies. It reduced an earlier list of 43 rules, and was reorganised in Haladyna & Rodriguez (2013). Designed for classroom assessment but considered useful for large-scale tests. — [UMN abstract](https://experts.umn.edu/en/publications/a-review-of-multiple-choice-item-writing-guidelines-for-classroom/); [Psicothema/Casu & Garcia](https://dx.doi.org/10.5093/psed2018a20)
- Content rules: test important content tied to the specifications; avoid trivial, opinion-based, trick, overly specific or overly general items; use vocabulary suited to the test-takers; keep items independent of each other. — [UNM summary of Haladyna et al. 2002](https://cpl.health.unm.edu/AssetListing/Writing-Questions-for-Learning-and-Assessment-2573/Rules-for-Multiple-Choice-Items-Haladyna-et-al-2002-5943)
- Stem rules: clear directions; put the main idea in the stem, not the options; word positively where possible; cut "window dressing"; if a negative is unavoidable, capitalise and bold it (NOT, EXCEPT). — same UNM summary
- Option rules: only one correct answer; vary key position; options independent and non-overlapping; homogeneous in content and grammar; about equal length; phrase positively; distractors plausible and based on common student errors; avoid absolute words (always/never) in distractors; no grammatical clues linking stem to one option; use NOTA cautiously; avoid or use AOTA sparingly; three options are adequate. — same UNM summary
- Empirical caveats: the guideline "Word the stem positively" had 63% endorsement and one study found no significant difference between positive and negative items. Three options vs five showed no significant difference. NOTA is the most contested rule (44% for, 48% against); in one study NOTA cut the proportion correct from 71% to 47% (p=0.044), meaning it adds a different cognitive demand. — [PMC3663625](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3663625) (one small study; do not generalise the exact numbers)
- Longest-option cue: the correct answer is often the one with more words and detail, which lets test-wise candidates choose without reading the stem. — [NCCPA test-wise clues handout](https://www.nccpa.net/wp-content/uploads/2021/06/Test-WiseCluesHandout.pdf) (surfaced in search; not fetched)

### Inferences
- For placement items, one reviewer-friendly rule is "fix the key position pattern, equalise length, and never use AOTA/NOTA at A1-A2" (NOTA is cognitively different from the target skill).

### Gaps
- I did not obtain the full text of Haladyna & Rodriguez (2013), so the exact numbering of the 31 guidelines is not reproduced; the list above is grouped from a secondary summary.

## 2. Cambridge / ALTE / Council of Europe item-writer guidance

### Takeaway
Professional bodies treat item writing as a team process: writer, then review by Chair, a second writer and an assessment manager, then edit, pretest and review of results. Quality criteria are relevance, level, specificity (no test-wiseness), objectivity (experts agree on the key), acceptability, language use, and one clear key tested in clear language.

### Cited Findings
- Cambridge item production cycle: commissioning, first submission, review by Chair + another writer + Assessment Manager, accept/reject/return, second submission, editing, pretesting on learners, review of pretest results, live use. — [Cambridge English, "The Cambridge approach to item writing" (slides)](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/webinars/the-cambridge-approch-to-item-writing-slides.pdf)
- Cambridge "important considerations": one clear key; clear and unambiguous phrasing; assess language not world knowledge; avoid unsuitable topics (war, religion, smoking, gambling, recreational drugs); avoid culturally specific references; consistent phrasing and length of options; no mathematical calculation. — same slides
- Cambridge examples of faulty items: (a) a listening item where the three options each paraphrase a different detail but the audio mentions all of them and does not clearly say which is "main" advice; (b) a time item (7.55 / 8.15 / 20.00) requiring arithmetic on the audio; (c) options differing in length and phrasing. They illustrate "items that don't work" vs "items that can be improved". — same slides (exact interpretation of each slide is mine; slides are sparse)
- RELANG/ECML (CEFR-linked) quality criteria: Relevance (tests only the intended skill; no reading load in a listening test, no grammar in a reading test), Level, Specificity (no unintended hints; avoid extreme words; avoid long-option cue; consistent option ordering; do not copy sentences from books), Objectivity (do different experts agree on the key; avoid gender/culture/experience bias; do not ask for opinions), Acceptability (clear instructions, no catch questions), Efficiency, Language use (short sentences, correct grammar, standardised question words, no double negatives). — [RELANG, Item construction: receptive skills](https://relang.ecml.at/Portals/51/relang-other-documents/Documentation/RELANG%20Item%20construction%20Receptive%20Skills.pdf)
- RELANG on MC options: plausible and mutually exclusive; grammatically linked to the stem; logical or alphabetical order; do not repeat elements of the text; no always/never; do not stand out by length, formulation or echo; no clues to other items; preferably positive. Distractors must be plausible to those who have not mastered the material and represent essential differences. — same RELANG
- RELANG advice for level: use expert teams, screeners, pretest data; avoid manipulating wording to change difficulty. — same RELANG
- Cambridge Item Writer Guidelines (as quoted by Cambridge research): each option, key and distractors, should belong to one coherent lexico-grammatical category (e.g. key "the" -> distractors "a", "an") so none stands out and because same-category words make effective distractors. — [Cambridge Assessment English, evidence-based approach to distractor generation (2019)](https://Www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/526186-research-notes-72.pdf)
- ALTE/CoE Manual for Language Test Development and Examining exists (2011; revised 2026) and covers the whole cycle from construct to item writing, review, pretesting and data management; I could not fetch it (HTTP 403). — [CoE manual page](https://www.coe.int/en/web/education/-/manual-for-language-test-development-and-examining-1)

### Inferences
- Listening rules often cited by item-writing trainers (information heard once and clearly; key not identifiable from world knowledge before listening; distractors that actually occur in the audio but do not answer the question) are consistent with the Cambridge slide examples but I did not find a primary text stating each as a rule.

### Gaps
- No primary Cambridge/ALTE text found that lists listening-specific rules verbatim (e.g. "distractors appear in audio", "pre-knowledge cue"). Treat those as standard practice, flagged unverified.
- Lancaster/British Council Hungary listening guidelines (https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/projects/examreform/Media/GL_Listening.pdf) appeared in search but were not read; worth a follow-up for listening rules.
- Alderson/Clapham/Wall, Green, Fulcher, Weir: not accessed (books).

## 3. Distractor design and red flags

### Takeaway
The best distractors come from real learner wrong answers. Cambridge's research found distractors built from Common Wrong Answers (CWAs) from open gap-fill pretests performed well (94.8% of 135 items passed statistical screening), and that the best distractor sets are same-category words (not odd ones).

### Cited Findings
- Cambridge generated MC gap-fill distractor sets from the most common wrong answers in an open gap-fill pretest: two distractors at A1-A2, three at B1 and above, never four. — [Cambridge research notes 2019](https://Www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/526186-research-notes-72.pdf)
- Effectiveness benchmark: a distractor should be picked by at least 5% (Kilgour & Tayyaba 2015) or 7% (Green 2013) of candidates. In the study, 11.9% of distractors fell below 5%. A repeated-word distractor ("ask you [you] some help") drew only 2.2% vs 14.7% in the open task, i.e. wrong answers that are natural in open format can be implausible as options. — same
- Converting open gap-fill to MC made items easier by about 9.5 Rasch points on average (difficulty is not preserved across formats). — same
- Non-standard CWA distractors appeared: proper nouns (e.g. "Maria", "Tokyo"), homophones (its/it's), wrong-category words (e.g. "I'm" for a verb gap, "Spanish" for article gap) that drew 10-29% of choices at A1-A2. So at low levels, cross-category distractors can work; at higher levels the category-consistency rule is still the Cambridge default. — same
- Typical error sources named in Haladyna: "common student errors" as the basis for distractors; and RELANG: plausibility for those who have not studied. — see sections 1-2
- Red flags (general item analysis sources): an arguably correct distractor creates a defensibility problem; a distractor with positive discrimination while the key is negative suggests a wrong key; implausible distractors inflate guessing. — [Assessment Systems, distractor analysis](https://assess.com/distractor-analysis-test-items/)

### Inferences
- Reviewer red flags for teen placement items (my synthesis, not from a single source): two defensible answers (including British vs American usage such as "have got"/"gotten", "at the weekend"/"on the weekend", "Do you have" vs "Have you got"); key predictable from stem grammar (a/an, is/are agreement that eliminates options without understanding); distractors that are wrong only because of spelling or typography; key that is the longest; absolute-word distractors; a gap-fill with several acceptable fillers while only one is keyed. These are standard practice but not individually cited here.
- Likely plausible-distractor sources for English learners: wrong tense/aspect, wrong preposition collocation, near-synonym with wrong collocation, L1 transfer errors, false friends, wrong word form (adjective/adverb), pronoun case. Source for the list as a taxonomy: not found (unverified; consistent with Cambridge CWA method).

### Gaps
- No source found on British/American dialect conflicts specifically in item review; flagged as expert-practice inference.

## 4. Review process and common rejection reasons

### Takeaway
Review is multi-stage and independent: key check, level check, fairness/sensitivity, then edit and pretest. Rejections cluster on ambiguous keys, level mismatch, testing world knowledge, and cues.

### Cited Findings
- Cambridge: reviewed by Chair, another writer and Assessment Manager; each can accept, reject or return; then edited and pretested. — [Cambridge slides](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/webinars/the-cambridge-approch-to-item-writing-slides.pdf)
- RELANG review questions: Is the item addressing the intended skill? Do different experts agree on the correct answer? Are text and question indicative of the CEFR level? Are there unintended hints? Is the item a catch question? Is it extremely hard/easy? — [RELANG](https://relang.ecml.at/Portals/51/relang-other-documents/Documentation/RELANG%20Item%20construction%20Receptive%20Skills.pdf)
- Sensitivity: avoid distressing/offensive topics and culture-specific references; content suitable for an international candidature. — [Cambridge slides](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/webinars/the-cambridge-approch-to-item-writing-slides.pdf)

### Inferences
- A practical reviewer routine: (1) answer the item blind without the key; (2) check each distractor is clearly wrong and the key clearly right; (3) read the stem alone and see whether the answer is guessable; (4) check lexis of stem and options is at or below the target CEFR level (except the tested item); (5) check cognates/false friends and topic familiarity for the learner population; (6) check teen suitability and sensitivity; (7) check length/position/grammar cues across the whole test.
- Readability of the stem should be below the tested level (RELANG "use short sentences", "language compatible with candidates' level").

### Gaps
- No published list of "top rejection reasons" with frequencies found.

## 5. Item analysis red flags once data exist

### Takeaway
Use facility (p), discrimination and distractor use together; none alone is decisive.

### Cited Findings
- Facility zone often cited 0.3-0.7 for language tests and discrimination above about 0.25; one study found no clear link between these indices and distractor efficiency. — search summary (indirect; see [FSU item analysis guide](https://testing.fsu.edu/sites/g/files/upcbnu2391/files/media/PDFs/ItemAnalysis.pdf), not fetched)
- Point-biserial: key should be positive (ideal +0.25 or more); distractors should be negative. — [U Toronto Quercus guide](https://archive.teaching.utoronto.ca/wp-content/uploads/2019/02/Multiple-Choice-Quercus-Quiz-Analysis-Reports-Guide.pdf)
- Another source expects about 40-95% correct for acceptable items and flags a positive-discriminating distractor as possible mis-key. — [assess.com](https://assess.com/distractor-analysis-test-items/)
- Distractor use: below 5% (Kilgour & Tayyaba) or 7% (Green) = non-functioning. — [Cambridge 2019](https://Www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/526186-research-notes-72.pdf)

### Inferences
- Placement tests need items spread across difficulty (not only 0.3-0.7), so low p at C1 or high p at A1 is expected; judge discrimination within the level band.
- Red flags: p near chance (about 0.33 for 3 options) with flat discrimination; negative discrimination for the key; one distractor chosen more than the key by high scorers; a distractor chosen by under 5%.

### Gaps
- The cited 0.3-0.7 range is from an aggregated secondary summary and conflicts with the 40-95% range from assess.com; thresholds differ by source and purpose.
