# Judging quality and level of reading and listening comprehension items (CEFR A1-B2, teens)

## 1. Reading: Cambridge text lengths, vocabulary profile and question types per level

### Takeaway
Official Cambridge pages give task formats and skill focus per level, but they publish almost no per-text word counts. Only the B2 First paper total (2,200-2,500 words) is official. Per-text lengths below come from third-party or sample-paper observation and are flagged. Skill focus climbs from "main message of a notice" (A2) to "detail and main idea" (B1) to "opinion, attitude, implication" (B2).

### Cited Findings
- A2 Key for Schools Reading Part 1: six short real-world texts (notices, messages), each with a 3-option MC question, read "for the main message". Part 2: seven questions matched to three short texts on one topic. Part 3: one long text, five 3-option MC questions, "for detailed understanding and main ideas". All MC items at A2 have 3 options. — [A2 Key for Schools Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/504506-a2-key-for-schools-handbook-2020.pdf)
- A2 Can Do: "CAN understand straightforward information within a known area"; "CAN understand the general meaning of a simplified textbook or article, reading very slowly". — [A2 Key for Schools Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/504506-a2-key-for-schools-handbook-2020.pdf)
- B1 Preliminary Reading: 6 parts, 32 questions, 45 min. Part 1 five short texts with MC. Part 3 one longer text with five MC. Part 4 gapped text, five removed sentences. Part 5 vocabulary gaps with MC options. — [B1 Preliminary exam format](https://cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/preliminary/exam-format/)
- B1 Preliminary Part 3 tests gist, global and detailed meaning, attitude, opinions and feelings, with 4 options (A-D). — [search summary of Cambridge B1 pages](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/qualifications/preliminary/format/)
- B2 First Reading and Use of English: total word count of the paper is 2,200-2,500 words across 7 parts, 52 questions. Part 5 is one text with six 4-option MC questions testing "detail, opinion, attitude, tone, purpose, main idea, gist, meaning from context, implication, text organisation features". — [Cambridge English First Handbook](https://cambridgeenglish.org/tr/Images/167791-cambridge-english-first-handbook.pdf)
- A2 Key for Schools Part 3 sample text is about 230 words (third-party count of the sample). — [open-exam-prep summary](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cambridge-a2-key-schools/reading-comprehension/reading-part-3-long-text); third-party, unverified against Cambridge.
- Cambridge's own teacher tips suggest training learners to read about 300 words in 3 min, then 2 min. This is practice guidance for the longest B1 text (Part 4 in the B1 guide), not a spec. — [B1 Preliminary for Schools reading guide](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/619044-developing-reading-skills-for-b1-preliminary-for-schools-a-guide-for-teachers.pdf)
- Cambridge's reading tips say unfamiliar words disrupt flow and that learners should keep going with the words they do understand. — [Tips for reading](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/562913-tips-for-reading-teachers.pdf)
- Cambridge reading papers are built to the Cambridge English Profile / English Vocabulary Profile word lists, which come from the Cambridge Learner Corpus. — [Text Inspector EVP page](https://textinspector.com/help/lexis-evp/)

### Inferences
- For a placement test, short texts of roughly 30-60 words at A1-A2, 80-150 at B1, 150-250 at B2 sit within the observed Cambridge pattern. This is my inference, not a published spec.
- Sentence length and share of out-of-level words are not published by Cambridge. Use the coverage thresholds in section 4 as the working rule.
- Question-type ladder to use in review:
  - A1/A2: locate a specific fact or message, 3 options.
  - B1: main idea plus detail, 3-4 options.
  - B2: attitude, opinion, implication, reference, 4 options.
  An A2-level item that needs implication is mis-levelled up. A B2 item that only asks for a stated fact is mis-levelled down.

### Gaps
- Official per-text word counts for A2 Key Parts 1-3 and B1 Parts 1 and 3 were not found. The Cambridge PDFs fetched did not state them.
- No official Cambridge figure for "percent of words beyond level" or mean sentence length. Not found.
- Cambridge Young Learners (Starters/Movers/Flyers) reading specs were not researched here.

## 2. Listening: speech rate, script length, replay, stem-audio relation, design flaws

### Takeaway
Cambridge plays every recording twice. Short items are about 30 seconds, long monologues and interviews 3-4 minutes. Cambridge publishes no words-per-minute figure. Research gives rough ranges, but they come from lecture and learner-speech corpora, not exam audio. Whether to play once or twice is a genuine trade-off. Playing once makes items about 10-14% harder in one study.

### Cited Findings
- A2 Key for Schools Listening: 25 questions, five parts, 13 recordings, about 30 min, each recording heard twice, 6 min to transfer answers. Part 1 is five short two-person recordings with picture options. Part 2 is a monologue with gap fill (words or numbers). Parts 3 and 4 are 3-option MC. Part 5 is matching. — [A2 Key for Schools Listening Overview](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/563255-A2-Key-for-Schools-Listening-Overview.pdf)
- A2 Part 1 "calls for an understanding of the gist of a monologue or dialogue containing neutral or less formal language", with attitudes, opinions and agreement possible. Candidates are told to listen for gist the first time and check on the second. — [A2 Key for Schools Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/504506-a2-key-for-schools-handbook-2020.pdf)
- The A2 handbook says recordings contain "a range of standard native-speaker accents". — [A2 Key for Schools Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/504506-a2-key-for-schools-handbook-2020.pdf)
- B1 Preliminary Listening: 25 questions, 4 parts, about 30 min plus 6 min transfer, each recording played twice. Part 1 seven short items with pictures. Part 2 six short dialogues testing main ideas. Part 3 monologue gap fill. Part 4 interview for detail and opinion. — [B1 Preliminary exam format](https://cambridgeenglish.org/exams-and-tests/preliminary/exam-format/)
- B2 First Listening: 4 parts, 30 questions, about 40 min, each part heard twice. Part 1 extracts of about 30 seconds each, one 3-option MC question per text. Part 3 five related monologues of about 30 seconds each. Parts 2 and 4 are 3-4 minute monologue or interview. Task focus includes feeling, attitude, opinion, purpose, agreement, gist and detail. — [Cambridge English First Handbook](https://cambridgeenglish.org/tr/Images/167791-cambridge-english-first-handbook.pdf)
- B2 First Listening: pauses are given before each task so candidates can read and predict. — [Cambridge English First Handbook](https://cambridgeenglish.org/tr/Images/167791-cambridge-english-first-handbook.pdf)
- Learner speech rate (picture description, not exam audio): B2 learners 118 wpm (sd 22), C1 learners 142 wpm (sd 20), native speakers 174 wpm (sd 34). — [Huang and Graf, Birmingham corpus conference 2017](https://www.birmingham.ac.uk/documents/college-artslaw/corpus/conference-archives/2017/general/paper229.pdf)
- Authentic lectures range about 110-190 wpm, with many at 160-170. TED-Ed averages 147.4 wpm. — [TED-Ed speech-rate study](https://e-journal.usd.ac.id/index.php/LLT/article/view/6414)
- Speech-rate experiments: one found 98 wpm helped comprehension and 58 wpm did not. Another found no significant effect across 130, 150, 170 and 190 wpm, with 150 wpm judged most acceptable. Griffiths (1990) found slower speech gave lower-intermediate learners few benefits and faster speech reduced comprehension. — [search summaries; Griffiths, see JALT proceedings](https://jalt-publications.org/archive/proceedings/2001/532.pdf). Mixed evidence, secondary summary, so treat the figures as indicative.
- Once vs twice: Fortune (2004, N=62) found once-heard tasks 10-14% more difficult, and most once-heard items discriminated better. Effects vary with ability and item type. Evidence is small-scale. Arguments from "real life" authenticity are inconclusive. Playing everything twice nearly doubles test time and limits the variety of texts. — [Glyn Jones, "Once or twice?" research summary](https://www.pearsonpte.com/content/dam/ELL/pte/pearsonpte/general/Listen-Once-Or-Twice--Glyn-Jones.pdf)
- Real speech is rapid and mostly unplanned, with false starts, fillers, pauses and connected speech (Buck's argument for authentic samples). — [summary of Buck, Assessing Listening](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/projects/examreform/Media/konyvPart2/chapter%2012.pdf)

### Inferences
- A speaking-rate guide for scripts, derived from the above and not a published exam spec: A1 about 90-110 wpm, A2 about 110-130, B1 about 130-150, B2 about 150-170. Mark this as the reviewer's working assumption.
- A once-played placement script of 25-60 words (about 10-30 seconds) matches the Cambridge 30-second extract. Because once-heard items are harder, a once-only test should either use easier-than-nominal content or accept a slightly lower measured level.
- Item-design checks for each listening item:
  - The answer should come in the middle of the audio, not in the first or last second.
  - Distractor numbers, times or names are said on purpose. At A2 and B1 this is the standard "trap": the speaker mentions several times and corrects one, so the key must be the final or corrected value. It is legitimate only if the audio makes the correct one unambiguous and the correction is a normal speech act ("Oh, sorry, it's half past four, not four").
  - The spoken audio should not repeat the key word verbatim in the correct option when a distractor option is also verbatim in the audio.
  - The stem should paraphrase the audio.

### Gaps
- No Cambridge-published wpm for exam recordings. The wpm-by-level table above is not sourced.
- The Tauroza and Allison (1990) and Griffiths (1990) numbers (for example 127 vs 188 wpm) could not be verified. Not used.
- The claim that "distractor numbers are a legitimate A2 trap" is based on the Cambridge task description (Part 2 asks for numbers and spelling; handbook tells learners to listen for numbers and times) but not on a Cambridge item-writer guideline. Treat as reasoned practice.

## 3. Principles for writing comprehension MC items

### Takeaway
The Haladyna, Downing and Rodriguez (2002) review is the standard source. Core rules are one correct answer, plausible homogeneous distractors, positive stems, and a stem that carries the main idea. Cambridge-specific rules (paraphrase, order of text) are standard ESL practice but I found them stated mainly in teaching guides.

### Cited Findings
- Haladyna et al. (2002) guidelines: put the main idea in the stem, not the options. Word stems positively. If a negative is unavoidable, emphasise it (capitalised or bold). Avoid unnecessary wording. Options should be independent, homogeneous in content and grammar, roughly equal in length, and positively phrased. Only one correct answer, with position varied. — [Rules for Multiple Choice Items, Haladyna et al. 2002](https://cpl.health.unm.edu/AssetListing/Writing-Questions-for-Learning-and-Assessment-2573/Rules-for-Multiple-Choice-Items-Haladyna-et-al-2002-5943)
- Three effective options are adequate. Quality of distractors matters more than their number. Write distractors from typical student errors. — [Haladyna et al. 2002, summary](https://bookdown.org/stefanmosjos/manualesitems/_book/haladyna-2002.html)
- Only a handful of guidelines have been tested empirically (number of options, "none of the above", negatively worded stems, option length). — [Haladyna et al. 2002, summary](https://bookdown.org/stefanmosjos/manualesitems/_book/haladyna-2002.html)
- Cambridge uses 3 options at A2 and 3-4 at B1/B2 (see sections 1-2 above).

### Inferences
- Reviewer checklist, combining the sources with the brief:
  1. The key paraphrases the text. If the correct option copies a phrase from the text while distractors do not, test-wise students pick it. Distractors can reuse text words but must be wrong in meaning.
  2. Each distractor is plausible and tied to a real text detail or common misreading.
  3. Exactly one key is defensible.
  4. Questions follow the order of the text (Cambridge short-text and multi-question parts do this; an inference from sample papers).
  5. The "cover the text" test: if a strong student can answer from general knowledge, the item measures knowledge, not comprehension.
  6. No negative stems (NOT/EXCEPT) at A1-B1. Haladyna allows them if emphasised.
  7. Options are the same length and grammatical form as one another, with no "all of the above".
  8. The stem does not give away the answer to another item.

### Gaps
- No Cambridge-published item-writer guide (the "Cambridge item writer guidelines") was found, so Cambridge-specific rules 1 and 4 are unverified as official requirements.

## 4. Estimating a passage's level quickly

### Takeaway
Classic readability scores (Flesch-Kincaid, Lexile) were built for native readers and predict L2 difficulty weakly. Vocabulary coverage against a CEFR-graded list is the better check. Research supports 95% known tokens as minimal and 98% as comfortable for reading, and about 95% as workable for listening.

### Cited Findings
- Brown (1998) found L1 readability indices only weakly related to EFL difficulty (23-30% of variance). Greenfield (1999) found they predicted EFL about as well as native difficulty. The two studies disagree. Both produced EFL-specific formulas. — [Readability formulas for EFL, JALT](https://jalt-publications.org/node/2622)
- Lexical coverage for reading: 98% of tokens is optimal, 95% minimal. About 8,000-9,000 word families for 98% in authentic texts, about 5,000 for 95%. — [Lexical threshold revisited, Frontiers/NFLRC](https://scholarspace.manoa.hawaii.edu/items/eaead47e-853e-4797-ba6f-596df150c1d7/full)
- Coverage for listening (van Zeeland and Schmitt 2012): comprehension scores out of 10 were 9.62 at 100% coverage, 8.22 at 98%, 7.65 at 95%, 7.35 at 90%. Suggested that 95% may be sufficient for listening with about 2,000-3,000 word families. — [Cambridge Language Teaching replication article](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/language-teaching/article/how-much-vocabulary-is-needed-to-use-english-replication-of-van-zeeland-schmitt-2012-nation-2006-and-cobb-2007/1D217A56A2E0056E67802A6A8360FDDE)
- Text Inspector tags each word, phrase, idiom and collocation in a text with its EVP level (A1-C2). EVP was built from the Cambridge Learner Corpus by Cambridge University Press and Cambridge English. — [Text Inspector EVP help](https://textinspector.com/help/lexis-evp/); [CEFR Level Checker](https://textinspector.com/cefr-level-checker/) (page itself returned 403 on fetch, so details taken from search summary only)

### Inferences
- Rule of thumb for a reviewer, derived from the thresholds: for a passage nominally at level X, at least 95% of tokens (98% for independent reading and for a placement test where a miss should reflect grammar or reading, not vocabulary) should be at level X or below, counting names and glossed words as known. More than about one unknown word in 20 (5%) is above level; more than one in 10 is two levels up.
- Practical workflow: paste into Text Inspector or the EVP search, read the A1/A2/B1/B2 percentage split, then check sentence length by hand. Use Flesch only as a sanity check on sentence and word length, never as the level decision.
- Cambridge's own reading advice (above) that learners should keep reading around unknown words fits the 95% figure: some unknowns are tolerated, but the key information must not depend on one.

### Gaps
- No published single rule for "mean sentence length by CEFR level" from Cambridge or the Council of Europe. Not found; any numbers (for example A1 under 8 words) would be unsourced.
- Text Inspector's exact output thresholds for assigning an overall CEFR label could not be read (403).
- Lexile limitations for L2 learners: only the general Brown/Greenfield evidence found; no Lexile-specific L2 source.

## 5. Synthetic vs studio voices, teens' real listening, replay policy for placement tests

### Takeaway
No source found compares TTS with studio voices for CEFR test items. The evidence available is about replay and speed in general. The sources support: avoid very slow artificial speech, include natural features modestly, and decide the replay rule explicitly because it shifts difficulty by about 10-14%.

### Cited Findings
- Once-played items were 10-14% harder than twice-played in Fortune (2004), with higher discrimination. — [Glyn Jones summary](https://www.pearsonpte.com/content/dam/ELL/pte/pearsonpte/general/Listen-Once-Or-Twice--Glyn-Jones.pdf)
- Everything played twice is an artificial convention that is "very far" from real-life conditions, yet some argue real life often allows repair or repetition. The evidence on which is more valid is inconclusive. — [Glyn Jones summary](https://www.pearsonpte.com/content/dam/ELL/pte/pearsonpte/general/Listen-Once-Or-Twice--Glyn-Jones.pdf)
- Buck: use authentic spoken texts, because real speech has hesitation, discourse structure and connected speech that scripted read-aloud lacks. — [Lancaster examreform chapter on listening](https://www.lancaster.ac.uk/fass/projects/examreform/Media/konyvPart2/chapter%2012.pdf)
- Much slower speech (58 wpm) did not help comprehension, while about 98 wpm did help in one study. — [search summary of speech-rate studies](https://e-journal.usd.ac.id/index.php/LLT/article/view/6414)
- Cambridge A2 Key for Schools recordings use "standard native-speaker accents", and candidates can raise a hand if they cannot hear. — [A2 Key for Schools Handbook](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/images/504506-a2-key-for-schools-handbook-2020.pdf)

### Inferences
- Because a recorded-once placement test is harder than Cambridge's twice-played format, offer replay for A1-A2 items or stay with once for an adaptive test and calibrate cut-scores on once-heard data. State the policy in the instructions.
- Accessibility: allow a replay button (logged) for students with hearing, processing or attention needs, and report that replay was used. Never silently substitute another voice.
- TTS risks to check by ear: flat prosody that removes natural stress on numbers and names, misread numbers and times ("4.30"), uniform pauses, and no connected speech. For this project the owner rule is to use recorded character voices (see CLAUDE.md), so these checks apply to the recorded clips: verify numbers and times are spoken as intended and that rate falls in the target band.
- Teen listening in real life is faster, overlapped and noisier than exam audio. A placement script at a controlled rate is a deliberate simplification.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed comparison of TTS and human voice on CEFR listening item difficulty was found.
- No specific guidance on replay policy for adaptive placement tests (as opposed to proficiency exams) was found. The advice above is inference.
