# Assessing very young language learners (ages 4-9): design rules for a 10-15 min tablet English placement test

Research depth note: ~14 tool calls. Several primary sources (Hasselgreen, McKay, Nikolov, PPVT/DIBELS technical manuals, Pearson YL, NAEYC) were NOT accessed directly; claims below are limited to what was actually retrieved. Gaps are listed per section.

## 1. Attention span, test length, breaks, rewards, fatigue detection

### Takeaway
Primary-source evidence on exact minutes is thin for ages 4-5. The best hard data are: ETS capped each TOEFL Primary section at 30 min (ages 8+) because attention is shorter than adults', and pilot data showed performance DECLINED on the longer form; the EGRA toolkit and WellComm are built around ~15 min; popular teacher sources cite ~10-18 min for 5-6 year-olds. A 10-15 min total test is defensible, but 4-5 year-olds should get shorter chunks (see inferences).

### Cited Findings
- ETS decided at conceptualization that max testing time per skill must not exceed 30 minutes because children have shorter attention spans than adults; in piloting two reading forms with different lengths, "students' performance declined on the longer test form," supporting fewer items. Final TOEFL Primary Reading/Listening: 30 scored MC items per test, 30 min each (target age 8+). — [Cho et al., ETS RM-16-02, Designing the TOEFL Primary Tests](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)
- TOEFL Primary Speaking is computer-delivered, about 20 min, 7-10 questions. (Secondary summary of the test; item counts per the ETS Global page.) — [ETS Global TOEFL Primary](https://www.etsglobal.org/al/en/test-type-family/toefl-primary-tests)
- TOEFL Primary research (secondary snippet): read-alouds' effect on attention and perceived item understanding was examined; memory load and ambiguity in task descriptions influenced young test takers' perceived difficulty. — [ETS TOEFL Primary research list](https://www.ets.org/pdfs/toefl/toefl-primary-research.pdf) (only a search snippet; not read in full)
- Cambridge Pre A1 Starters Listening is about 20 min, 4 parts, 20 questions. — [Cambridge English Starters format / HAU summary](https://www.hau.gr/en-us/exams/language-certifications/university-of-cambridge/pre-a1-starters) and [Cambridge sample papers](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/es/Images/722535-cambridge-english-young-learners-sample-papers-volume-1.pdf)
- The EGRA toolkit was designed to capture early reading skills in a ~15 minute assessment. — [USAID/RTI EGRA search result](https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00TZM8.pdf) (snippet-level)
- WellComm (UK speech/language screen) takes 10-15 min per child, depending on pace, number of levels, and breaks the child needs. — [GL Assessment WellComm support](https://support.gl-assessment.co.uk/knowledge-base/assessments/wellcomm-support/on-the-day-of-the-test)
- Popular teacher-training source: attention of 5-6 year-olds about 12-18 min with fun activities ("children usually concentrate 10 to 15 minutes"). Low-quality aggregator, flagged as weak evidence. — [TKT young learners study guide](https://open-exam-prep.com/study-guides/cambridge-tkt-young-learners/knowledge-young-learners/children-development-characteristics)
- Remote play-based sessions with ~600 preschoolers (3-6) ran up to 30 min with engagement maintained via playful interaction; caregiver presence helped redirect attention. — [Frontiers in Public Health 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1376090/full)
- Disengagement detection practice (adult/school CAT): NWEA MAP flags a response as a rapid guess when response time is well below the item's typical time; a student is "disengaged" after 3 successive rapid guesses. — [NWEA via search result](https://www.nwea.org/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/24854) (snippet; not verified in the original)

### Inferences
- Budget by age: 4-5: ~8-10 min, ~16-20 items in 2 short "levels" with a character break between; 6-7: ~10-12 min, ~24-30 items; 8-9: ~12-15 min, ~30 items (TOEFL Primary's 30 items/30 min is much slower than 1 item/25 s; tablet picture items can run ~15-25 s each). These item counts are my design suggestions, not published norms.
- ETS's finding that longer = worse performance supports early stopping rules (adaptive, stop on ceiling) over fixed long forms.
- Rewards should be non-contingent on correctness (stars for tapping/finishing, not for right answers), otherwise feedback distorts results/teaches the answer. (Reasoned; no source retrieved on feedback distortion.)
- Fatigue/disengagement flags to log: response time under ~1 s (before audio ends), 3+ consecutive fast responses, same-position tapping streak, accuracy collapse in the last third vs first, long idle gaps. Thresholds must be piloted.

### Gaps
- No retrieved source gives validated attention-span minutes for 4, 6-7, 8-9 year-olds in test settings (the often-quoted "age + 1-2 minutes" rule was not found with a citable source).
- NAEYC guidance, Hasselgreen, McKay, Nikolov not accessed.
- No evidence retrieved on whether game-like feedback distorts scores in young learners.

## 2. Response formats for non-readers (point/tap, options count, pictures)

### Takeaway
Single-selection picture/audio multiple choice is the standard format (TOEFL Primary, Starters Part 3 uses 3 pictures). General MC research (Rodriguez 2005) finds three options are generally optimal; for pre-readers, 3 pictures with well-chosen distractors reduces load vs 4 at little reliability cost. ETS found matching a picture to a word is very easy and gives little information for more proficient children, so ceiling is reached quickly.

### Cited Findings
- TOEFL Primary Reading/Listening are single-selection MC items; task types include "match picture to word" and "match picture to one of three words" (listening and reading blueprints show 3-option picture tasks, 6-7 items per task type). — [ETS RM-16-02](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)
- ETS: picture-matching is inherently simple and gives proficient learners little chance to show ability, while long-text comprehension is too hard for low-proficiency learners; hence two difficulty levels (Step 1 for low-mid, Step 2 for mid-high). — [ETS RM-16-02](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)
- ETS prototyping: teachers preferred communication-oriented tasks over discrete skills; a phonemic-awareness (sound-to-letter) prototype was dropped as not extensively used on the final test. — [ETS RM-16-02](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)
- Cambridge Pre A1 Starters Listening Part 3: five short conversations, each with a question and three pictures (A, B, C). Part 4 involves colouring objects per what is heard. — [Cambridge English Starters Digital test format](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/fr/exams-and-tests/starters-digital/test-format) and [HAU summary](https://www.hau.gr/en-us/exams/language-certifications/university-of-cambridge/pre-a1-starters)
- Rodriguez (2005), meta-analysis of 80 years of research: three options are optimal for MC items in most settings; distractor quality, not number, protects against undeserved credit; blind guessing by low-ability students is a validity threat on speeded tests. — [summary at Tips for Teachers](https://tipsforteachers.substack.com/p/research-bite-20-three-options-are) (secondary; original not read)

### Inferences
- With 3 options chance is 33% vs 25% with 4; because a short test has few items, use 4 pictures only for the easiest words and use adaptive stop rules plus consistency pairs (same word tested twice in different frames) to discount lucky guesses. Alternatively 3 options + a "no" distractor free design. Judgment call, no source.
- Practice rules (design suggestions, unsourced): 2-3 spoken-instruction practice items with explicit feedback and a "tap again" retry; targets at least ~1.5 cm / large tap zones, evenly spaced, randomized position, no option position bias; distinct, culturally neutral, single-concept pictures; no text anywhere; replay-audio button allowed once and logged.
- Avoid abstract prompts for 4-5s (ETS notes memory load and ambiguity inflate perceived difficulty).

### Gaps
- Hasselgreen, Bailey, Papp, McKay, Pearson YL Tests not accessed; no source found for tap-target size or picture rules specifically.
- No quantified 3 vs 4 option guessing data for children.

## 3. Pre-literacy / early literacy measures (separating cannot-read vs reads-a-little)

### Takeaway
Standard frameworks use letter identification (names and sounds), phonological awareness, familiar-word reading, nonword reading and passage fluency (EGRA); DIBELS 8th ed. uses Letter Naming Fluency, Phonemic Segmentation Fluency, Nonsense Word Fluency, Word Reading Fluency, ORF, Maze, and dropped Initial Sound Fluency. These are oral-response, individually administered, timed (≈1 min each) tests; for an unsupervised tablet test they must be converted to tap-based receptive versions. Cross-script validity is poorly documented.

### Cited Findings
- EGRA standard subtasks: letter identification (names and sounds, upper and lower case), familiar word reading, nonword reading, oral reading fluency with comprehension (passage about 60 words); toolkit is flexible so users select subtasks. — [EGRA toolkit revision (UIS/USAID)](https://tcg.uis.unesco.org/wp-content/uploads/sites/4/2022/11/WG_GAML_17_UISAID-EGRA-Toolkit-Revision_final.pdf) and [USAID](https://pdf.usaid.gov/pdf_docs/PA00TZM8.pdf)
- DIBELS 8: measures are LNF (risk indicator), PSF (phonological awareness), NWF, WRF, ORF, Maze; no ISF/FSF; LNF accounts for letter frequency in upper/lower case; each sub-test about one minute. — [DIBELS 8 vs Previous Editions](https://dibels.amplify.com:443/docs/dibels/DIBELS-8-vs-Previous-Editions-Admin-Scoring.pdf) and [Amplify](https://dibels.amplify.com/assessment/dibels-eighth-edition)
- Arabic: syllable awareness and letter knowledge are early reading predictors; visual attention span contributes to Arabic reading via letter knowledge. Transfer studies of Arabic-English phonological awareness exist but are few; many L2 literacy studies only cover shared (Latin) scripts. — [Frontiers in Psychology 2025](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/psychology/articles/10.3389/fpsyg.2025.1628051/pdf), [Asaad & Eviatar 2013](https://newiipdm.haifa.ac.il/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/AsaadEviatar2013b.pdf) (snippet-level only)

### Inferences
- Tap-based ladder for a tablet (suggested, ~2-3 items each; 12-15 items total): (1) print concept: tap the letter vs a picture vs scribble; (2) hear a letter name -> tap among 3 letters; (3) hear a sound -> tap the letter; (4) initial-sound matching with pictures (no text; "which starts with /b/?"), rhyme; (5) hear a word -> tap the matching printed word among 3 (CVC like cat/dog/sun); (6) hear a short sentence -> tap matching sentence-or-picture only for the top band. Stop the ladder at the first level with <=1/3 correct.
- Phonological awareness (rhyme/initial sound) in English still works for Arabic-L1 children without needing Latin print, so it separates "can hear English sounds" from "can decode"; letter-name items confound by script exposure, so treat low letter scores as "no Latin literacy yet" not low ability.
- Pre-literate in L1 means do not use any text instruction; use only audio + icons.

### Gaps
- No item-count recommendations or reliability numbers for these subtasks were retrieved; DIBELS/EGRA technical manuals not read.
- No source on PALS or YARC retrieved; Cambridge Pre A1 reading tasks not read in detail.
- No cross-script (Arabic vs Latin) validity guidance for English letter-name tests found.

## 4. Oral comprehension without speaking (receptive vocab, TPR, sentence-picture, adaptive start/stop)

### Takeaway
PPVT-style receptive vocabulary with picture-pointing is the model: start at an age-based point, find a basal, stop at a ceiling. PPVT-4 uses 12-item sets with basal = 11-12 correct and ceiling = 8+ errors in a set; PPVT-5 changed to continuous items with basal 3 consecutive correct and ceiling 6 consecutive incorrect, shortening administration.

### Cited Findings
- PPVT-4: basal when a 12-item set has 1 or no errors; ceiling when a set has 8 or more errors. — [Manchester PPVT results sheet / CLOSER guide](https://closer.ac.uk/cross-study-data-guides/cognitive-measures-guide/explore/ncds/multi-age/ppvt-revised) (search summary)
- PPVT-5: continuous item sets, basal 3 consecutive correct, ceiling 6 consecutive incorrect; age-based start points (e.g. ages 2.5-3 start Set 1, age 4 start Set 2). — [Pearson PPVT-5 and EVT-3 what's changed](https://www.pearsonclinical.com.au/content/dam/school/global/clinical/au/assets/ppvt-5/ppvt-5-and-evt-3-whats-changed.pdf) and [PPVT-5 sample report](https://www.PearsonClinical.ca/content/dam/school/global/clinical/us/assets/ppvt-5/ppvt-5-sample-score-summary-report.pdf)
- TOEFL Primary listening tasks include dialogue/short talk comprehension with picture options (listening 30 items). — [ETS RM-16-02](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)

### Inferences
- Adopt a PPVT-5-like rule: start by self-reported age band, stop after 5-6 consecutive misses (consistent with a 10 min budget); a 4-year-old beginner will hit the ceiling in ~8-10 items.
- Content ladder for beginners (suggested): colours/shapes/numbers 1-5 -> animals/body/food nouns -> action verbs (TPR: "touch your nose", tap pictured action) -> counting 6-10 -> prepositions (in/on/under) -> sentence-picture matching (He is eating / She is eating) -> short dialogue question. 20-30 total items; fewer than ~20 gives placement bands (3-4 bands), not precise scores. Item counts unsourced.

### Gaps
- No documented reliability by item count for PPVT-like placement in children; PPVT manual not accessed.
- No source on TPR as an assessment format retrieved.

## 5. Risks of remote / unsupervised testing and mitigations

### Takeaway
Remote tablet assessment of preschoolers is feasible, but caregiver presence is needed for engagement and technical help; data quality problems (technical and interpersonal) were frequent, and some remote tablet results did not correlate with parent report. Parent over-help is not quantified in what I retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Remote assessment is increasingly recognized as valid and reliable for young children; preschoolers show high task completion and accuracy on tablets in the field. — [Frontiers in Public Health 2024](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1376090/full)
- Caregivers helped engage children, positioned the device, redirected attention, and clarified unclear responses (note: the same help is a validity risk in a test). — [same](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1376090/full)
- Difficulties completing remote tablet assessment were frequent, both technical and interpersonal; in some studies remote tablet results did not correlate with parent reports; children from about 2 years can interact with tablets. — [search summary of remote tablet studies](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11208674/) (PMC page blocked; not read in full)
- Rapid-guess/disengagement detection by response time is established in CAT practice (see section 1). — [NWEA](https://www.nwea.org/blog/wp-json/wp/v2/posts/24854)

### Inferences
- Mitigations (design suggestions): spoken+visual "please sit with your child, let them tap; don't tell the answer" gate (a parent checkbox is cheap); log time-to-first-tap, tap coordinates (a repeated exact corner = random), position bias, replays, background-noise level via mic if consented; consistency items (same word twice); microphone-free core scoring so noise does not matter; confidence band on results with "teacher will confirm"; allow resume.
- Report a result confidence flag (high/medium/low) instead of silently placing a low-engagement child.

### Gaps
- No quantified rates of parent help or random tapping in young-learner L2 tests; no NAEYC guidance retrieved.

## 6. Automatic speech recognition for children's speech

### Takeaway
ASR is markedly worse for young children: roughly 5% WER for adults vs 11-18% for children overall, 15-21% for ages 6-10 and up to ~35% for ages 4-6 with state-of-the-art models; noise can double child error rates. L2 child speech (accented, code-switching, beginner single words) is harder still. A short recorded sample for human review is the safer scoring route.

### Cited Findings
- Adult ~5% WER; children 11-18%; ages 6-10: 15-21%; kindergarten (4-6): up to 35%; noise can double children's error rates; read-aloud gives lower WER than conversational speech; performance stabilizes after ~10, approaching adult around 12-13. Studies: Shivakumar 2020 (~34% at 4-6), Kathania 2022 (~35%), Johnson 2022. — [The Learning Agency, Closing the child speech recognition gap](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)
- Whisper: about 3% WER for adults reading prepared speech but about 25% on child voices in comparable conditions. — [The Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/the-cutting-ed/article/how-speech-recognition-systems-struggle-with-childrens-voices/) (snippet)
- Age (physiology) and number of words in the audio have the largest effect on child ASR errors, then background noise and pronunciation ability. — [arXiv 2502.08587 causal analysis](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.08587)
- Fine-tuning on small diverse child datasets can cut error 20-96%; parameter-efficient fine-tuning gave 12-30% WER reduction. — [arXiv 2502.08587](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2502.08587) and [The Learning Agency](https://the-learning-agency.com/guides-resources/closing-the-child-speech-recognition-gap-evidence-limitations-and-paths-forward/)
- Non-native children's speech has higher acoustic/lexical variability and code-switching; engines need child adaptation. Pronunciation-assessment systems for 10-12 year-old Korean ESL children (17,800 utterances, 300 speakers) reach teacher-level agreement in research settings. — [LREC 2020 KESL dataset](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2020.lrec-1.47.pdf) (snippet-level)
- Child speech intelligibility is only ~55% at age 3 and ~70% at age 4 (human listeners). — [Frontiers 2024 guidelines](https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/public-health/articles/10.3389/fpubh.2024.1376090/full)
- ETS prototyped speaking as computer-recorded vs face-to-face; the final TOEFL Primary Speaking is computer-delivered with human-style rubric scoring of recorded responses (not ASR-scored). — [ETS RM-16-02](https://www.ets.org/Media/Research/pdf/RM-16-02.pdf)

### Inferences
- For a beginner 4-9 test, do not auto-score speech for placement; record 1-2 short prompts ("What's your name?", "Say what you see") with parental consent and a skip option, and let a teacher review. ASR could be used only to flag "silent/empty recording" or give non-scoring encouragement.
- Opt-in recording and child-data/privacy rules (COPPA/GDPR-K) need legal review; not researched here.

### Gaps
- No WER figures specific to non-native (L2) 4-9 year-olds' single-word English.
- Primary papers (Shivakumar, Kathania) not read; figures taken from a secondary summary.
