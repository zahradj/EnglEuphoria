# L1-specific errors, fairness and bias for teen placement-test items (Arabic, French, Spanish, Italian, Turkish; A1-B2)

Note on evidence: most per-L1 sources found are secondary (TEFL/editing blogs, small EFL error-analysis papers, mostly adult/university learners). Swan & Smith "Learner English" was only seen second-hand via papers. No source found is specific to ages 10-17. Items under "Inferences" are expert-knowledge generalisations NOT verified by a fetched source; the teacher should judge them.

## 1. Most common grammar/vocabulary errors per L1 (realistic distractors)

### Takeaway
Each L1 has a few well-documented transfer errors that make natural distractors: Arabic and Turkish (articles, copula/word order, prepositions); French/Italian/Spanish (present perfect vs past, prepositions, missing subject "it", false friends, French uncountable plurals).

### Cited Findings
- Arabic: no indefinite article; the definite article has a different range of uses; most common error is "the" for generic reference (Swan & Smith 2001, cited second-hand) — [Asian EFL Journal](https://asian-efl-journal.com/monthly-editions-new/article-errors-in-the-english-writing-of-advanced-l1-arabic-learners-the-role-of-transfer/index.htm); [Libyan article study](https://dspace.elmergib.edu.ly/handle/123456789/1284)
- Arabic: no overt present-tense copula; copula omission is among the most frequent errors of Arab EFL writers, including Saudi learners — [Academicus paper](https://albanica.al/index.php/academicus/article/view/5026); [TPLS paper](https://tpls.academypublication.com/index.php/tpls/article/download/9833/9819/38851)
- Arabic: only two tenses (perfect/imperfect) so progressive and perfective forms are hard; prepositions a persistent problem; p/b substitution (*beoble, *bubil); vowel-spelling analogy errors (*languidge, *maney) — [Llexi Arabic](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-arabic-speakers/); [Arab students' preposition errors](https://repozytorium.amu.edu.pl/handle/10593/2696)
- Turkish: five most frequent errors in a Turkish EFL error analysis were articles, word choice, prepositions, word order, subject-verb agreement — [DOAJ, Educational Process Int. J. 2017](https://doaj.org/article/d6c3cb9e5247484b810aef5d80f6c14e)
- Turkish: SOV order gives delayed verbs ("*I the budget reviewed"); no definite article; gender-neutral "o" causes he/she mix-ups; suffixes for time/place give "*in Monday"; nouns stay singular after numerals ("*five engineer") — [Llexi Turkish](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-turkish-speakers/); [elon.io word order](https://elon.io/grammar/turkish/common-mistakes/word-order)
- Turkish: relative clauses precede the noun in Turkish, so English post-nominal relatives are hard — [Ontesol](https://ontesol.com/blog/teaching-english-abroad/turkey/tefl-turkey-teaching-grammar/)
- French: "I work here since two years" (present perfect); "depend of", "interested by"; "informations/advices"; "I am agree"; he/she for objects — [Llexi French](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-french-speakers/); [Scribendi](https://www.scribendi.com/advice/french_speakers_common_english_errors.en.html)
- French false friends: actuellement = currently (not actually), assister à = attend (not assist), sympathique = friendly (not sympathetic) — [Llexi French](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-french-speakers/)
- Italian: present perfect with finished-time words ("*I have finished it yesterday"); double negatives ("*we don't have no money"); "the phone of Anika"; missing subject "it" ("*Is working"); in/on/at ("*in Monday"); Italian a/in/da vs English "to" — [Llexi Italian](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-italian-speakers/); [EditorWorld Italian](https://www.editorworld.com/article/common-english-writing-mistakes-italian-writers-make)
- Italian false friends: actual/attuale, fame (hunger), libreria (bookshop), confront/confrontare; "brave" is listed by one source as meaning "good" in Italian — [Llexi Italian](https://llexi.com/l1-errors/common-english-errors-italian-speakers/); [Daily Italian Words](https://dailyitalianwords.com/?p=38424)
- Spanish: subject omission ("*Is important"); adjective after noun ("*a house beautiful"); "all this problems" (this/these); one preposition "en" for in/on/at — [Verbling](https://verbling.com/articles/post/5-common-mistakes-spanish-speakers-make-); [Stanford (Hakuta) archive](https://web.stanford.edu/~hakuta/www/archives/syllabi/E_CLAD/sfusd_cult_03/sonny_matt/CommonLanguageErrors.html)
- Spanish false friends: embarazada = pregnant (not embarrassed); librería = bookshop (library = biblioteca) — [Mosaic](https://engage.mosaicbc.org/blog/language-of-the-day-false-friends-1); [FluentU](https://fluentu.com/blog/spanish/spanish-english-false-cognates)

### Inferences (unsourced expert generalisations - verify)
- Distractor ideas by L1: Arabic = zero copula/zero article, generic "the", b/p spelling; Turkish = missing article, verb-final order, "*in Monday", singular after numbers; French = "*since two years", "*informations", "*depend of"; Spanish = "*Is raining", "*I have 12 years" (tener + years; widely known, not fetched), "*this problems"; Italian = "*I have seen him yesterday", "*in Monday", double negation.
- Missing "it" and double negation are good A1-A2 distractors for Spanish/Italian; copula and article omission for Arabic/Turkish at A1-A2.

### Gaps
- No primary Swan & Smith text; no teen-specific data; no Cambridge English Profile / Learner Corpus pages retrieved, so level assignment (A1 vs B2) is unsupported. Arabic relative-pronoun errors (resumptive pronouns), subject-verb agreement, and Spanish/Arabic uncountable nouns not confirmed by a fetched source.

## 2. Cognates, false friends and item difficulty by L1 (DIF)

### Takeaway
Cognate overlap inflates scores of Romance-L1 test takers on lexical items and makes those items more likely to show DIF; practical handling is expert review per L1, DIF analysis when samples allow, and not keying vocabulary items on cognates.

### Cited Findings
- Performance on lexical tests may be inflated for examinees whose L1 shares many cognates with the target language; accuracy is higher on cognates — [TELUQ repository](https://r-libre.teluq.ca/1398)
- Items that are cognates for one language group are more likely to show DIF (French vs Polish speakers, PPVT) — [Language Testing, SAGE](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0265532212459028)
- DIF results depend on examinees' first language; ETS report on L1 and DIF — [ETS 2009](https://www.ets.org/research/policy_research_reports/publications/report/2009/hvrt.html)
- Potential L1-related bias in items of an English proficiency test — [Coniam & Lee 2021](https://cdone.languagecert.org/-/media/feature/documents/research-team/publications-2021/coniam-d--lee-t-2021-potential-bias-in-languagecert-iesol-items.ashx)

### Inferences (unsourced)
- Trivially easy for Romance speakers but not Arabic/Turkish: hotel, restaurant, police, family, animal, music, doctor, hospital, important, difficult, intelligent, university, information, decision, accident. Turkish and Arabic also have many loans (otel, polis, telefon, televizyon), so check each key against all five L1s rather than assuming.
- A false-friend distractor is a trap only for the relevant Romance group and is neutral for Arabic/Turkish: flag as L1-dependent difficulty.
- Small platform: use expert per-L1 review now; split item statistics by L1 once each group has enough responses.

### Gaps
- No Cambridge English Profile page on cognate handling; no source for minimum DIF sample sizes.

## 3. American vs British English in an American-English placement test

### Takeaway
Grammar differences (gotten, collective nouns, on/at the weekend) can produce a second defensible answer; avoid them as keys and accept both variants wherever the item depends on variety.

### Cited Findings
- AmE "on the weekend" vs BrE "at the weekend" — [Verbling](https://verbling.com/articles/post/eight-differences-between-british-and-am)
- AmE get/got/gotten vs BrE get/got/got; "have got" exists in AmE but means "have" — [Verbling](https://verbling.com/articles/post/eight-differences-between-british-and-am)
- Collective nouns: BrE singular or plural ("the team are"), AmE singular — [Verbling](https://verbling.com/articles/post/eight-differences-between-british-and-am); [OneStopEnglish](https://www.onestopenglish.com/support-for-teaching-grammar/differences-in-american-and-british-english-grammar-article/152820.article)

### Inferences (unsourced; teacher to confirm)
- Unsafe as keys: have got/have; gotten/got; collective-noun agreement; at/on the weekend; "Have you got" vs "Do you have"; present perfect vs past simple with just/already/yet (AmE accepts past simple); different from/than/to; "Monday through Friday"; in hospital/in the hospital.
- Spelling pairs (color/colour, realize/realise, skepticism/scepticism, center/centre, math/maths): accept both in a diagnostic; never penalise either.
- Vocabulary pairs (soccer/football, movie/film/cinema, fall/autumn, mom/mum, apartment/flat, cookie/biscuit, elevator/lift, vacation/holiday, fries/chips, trash/rubbish): most European and Turkish teens were taught BrE, so avoid them as keys; "football" is a special trap.
- Ms./Miss: use first names or "Ms." to avoid the issue.
- Safe: structures identical in both varieties (articles, basic tenses, prepositions of place, countable/uncountable).

## 4. Bias and sensitivity guidance for tests for minors in many countries

### Takeaway
ETS and Cambridge practice: avoid stereotypes and content that may upset or disadvantage a group, run a sensitivity review by a cross-cultural panel, and complement it with DIF analysis.

### Cited Findings
- ETS reviews all products for non-offensive, non-stereotyping content, trains item writers, and its 2022 Guidelines recommend diverse representation and diverse writer/reviewer pools — [ETS fairness guidelines](https://www.ets.org/about/fairness/guidelines.html); [ETS international fairness review](https://www.de.ets.org/content/dam/ets-org/pdfs/about/fairness-review-international.pdf)
- Cambridge fairness: avoid content that may upset groups (gender roles, religion, socio-economic groups, age, ethnic groups); items get sensitivity review by a cross-cultural panel (accept/flag/reject) — [Cambridge item-writing slides](https://assets.cambridgeenglish.org/webinars/the-cambridge-approch-to-item-writing-slides.pdf); [Studies in Language Testing 9](https://www.cambridgeenglish.org/Images/734974-studies-in-language-testing-volume-9.pdf)
- Common avoid-lists: politics, religion, war, smoking, gambling, drug use, ethnicity, personal characteristics (height, weight, age, skin colour) — [DataCamp guide (secondary)](https://instructor-support.datacamp.com/en/articles/6248286-fairness-and-topics-to-avoid); [Paragon fairness guidelines](https://www.paragontesting.ca/wp-content/uploads/2018/10/ParagonFairnessGuidelines_Abridged_1809.pdf)

### Inferences (unsourced expert judgement)
- For teens also avoid: dating/romance, alcohol, pork/ham/bacon/wine/BBQ as food examples, Halloween/Christmas/Valentine/Thanksgiving/Easter, dogs as pets in a default role, swimwear scenes, brands, regional sports (baseball, cricket, American football), school-system specifics (prom, grades, dorm), wealth markers (own phone/car, foreign holidays), assumed two-parent families, gender stereotypes (girls shop, boys football).
- Safe topics: school subjects, daily routine, hobbies (music, drawing, games, swimming), animals, weather, public transport, neutral food (rice, bread, fruit, cheese, tea, eggs, pasta), technology, nature, museums.
- Names recognisable and neutral across Arab, European and Turkish teens: Adam, Sara, Maya, Lina, Nora, Leo, Mia, Alex, Daniel, Emma, Tom, Sami, Ali, Ben, Zoe. Avoid religion-marked names (Muhammad, Jesus), mixing nationalities with stereotyped roles, and names tied to holidays.

### Gaps
- ETS 2022 guideline text not fetched; the name sets and topic lists are expert suggestions, not sourced.

## 5. Test-wiseness cues and how to detect them

### Takeaway
Classic MCQ research (Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez) treats unequal option length, "all of the above", grammatical cues and non-homogeneous options as flaws that let students guess without knowledge.

### Cited Findings
- Haladyna, Downing & Rodriguez (2002) validated 31 item-writing guidelines (from 43); revised by Haladyna & Rodriguez (2013) — [Psicologia Educativa 2018](https://journals.copmadrid.org/psed/art/psed2018a20); [UNM summary](https://cpl.health.unm.edu/AssetListing/Writing-Questions-for-Learning-and-Assessment-2573/Rules-for-Multiple-Choice-Items-Haladyna-et-al-2002-5943)
- "All of the above" should not be an option (students guess or avoid it); "none of the above" used cautiously or avoided — [Nova Southeastern guidelines](https://cms.nova.edu/hpded/ctl/forms/item-writing-guidelines.pdf); [KU Special Connections](https://specialconnections.ku.edu/assessment/quality_test_construction/teacher_tools/multiple_choice_questions)
- Options should be independent, homogeneous in content and grammar, roughly equal in length, positively phrased; the key should not be longest — [UConn CETL](https://cetl.uconn.edu/resources/assessment-of-learning/assessment-design/developing-multiple-choice-questions/); [Nova guidelines](https://cms.nova.edu/hpded/ctl/forms/item-writing-guidelines.pdf)

### Inferences (unsourced detection checklist)
- Grammatical cue: only the key fits the stem's a/an, is/are, verb form. Test: read every option into the stem; if one is ungrammatical for non-target reasons, flag.
- Repetition: key shares a word with the stem, or is the option most similar to the others. Test: count overlapping words.
- Longest/most specific/hedged option is the key; absolutes (always, never) are wrong. Test: compare lengths; key should be longest in no more than about a quarter to a third of items.
- Position: keys cluster on B/C. Test: tally positions across the test and randomise.
- Odd one out: three similar options and one different. Test: sort options by form.
- Cross-item clues and "answerable without the passage". Test: a teacher answers blind, without passage or stem.
- Implausible distractors (non-words, wrong part of speech): replace with L1-realistic errors (section 1), one error type each; check distractor selection rates and discrimination after pilot.

### Gaps
- No research found on test-wise strategies of non-native teens specifically; the 2002 Haladyna paper itself was seen only through summary pages.
